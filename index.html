Why jigsaw pieces cannot slide apart

Rotem Assouline, Itai Benjamini and Claude Fable

Last updated: October 24, 2026

Illustration by Itai Benjamini.

The following question arose when one of us (Itai) was assembling a two-piece jigsaw puzzle with his toddler granddaughter Ayala. To separate two adjacent pieces of a jigsaw puzzle, one has to lift one of them off the table: no amount of sliding and turning in the plane will do. Which geometric property of the cut between the two pieces is responsible for this?

The other modes allow only a single straight slide (along the dashed line), or a single turn about a pin. The red region shows where \(q\) would overlap \(p\).

The question

Mathematically, a jigsaw puzzle is a bounded region of the plane, the board, divided into finitely many pieces: disjoint connected open sets whose closures cover the board, each bounded by finitely many piecewise-smooth closed curves. (A piece may have holes, into which other pieces fit.) Two pieces \(p\) and \(q\) are adjacent if their closures touch. We assume that they meet along a single piecewise-smooth curve \(\gamma=\overline p\cap\overline q\), their interface; it is either an arc or a closed curve.

A board cut into six pieces. The pieces \(p\) and \(q\) are adjacent, and their interface \(\gamma\) is drawn thick.

We parametrize \(\gamma\) by arclength, oriented so that \(q\) lies to its left, and at every point where \(\gamma\) is smooth we let \(\dot\gamma\) be the unit tangent and \(n\) the unit normal pointing into \(q\). Thus \(n=\dot\gamma^\perp\), where \(v^\perp\) denotes the vector \(v\) rotated by \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise.

The interface \(\gamma\), traversed with \(q\) on its left, and the unit tangent \(\dot\gamma\) and unit normal \(n\) at one of its points.

We keep \(p\) fixed and move \(q\) by a continuous family of rigid motions of the plane, starting at the identity. The motion is admissible if the moving piece never overlaps \(p\): the pieces may stay in contact and slide along each other, but their interiors may not meet. If no admissible motion moves \(q\), the pieces are interlocked.

Left: \(q\) has moved without overlapping \(p\). Right: \(q\) overlaps \(p\) (in red), which is not allowed. The dashed curve shows the original position of \(q\).

Two kinds of motions are easy to test: sliding \(q\) in a fixed direction, and turning it about a fixed pivot. We first find the conditions under which these fail, and then show that, under a mild assumption, they are the only motions that need to be tested.

A slide of \(q\) in the direction of a vector \(w\), and a turn of \(q\) about a pivot \(z\).

Sliding

Suppose we slide \(q\) in the direction of a vector \(w\). At a point of the interface where \(n\cdot w\lt 0\), the motion immediately pushes \(q\) into \(p\). If, on the other hand,

\[ n\cdot w\ge 0\qquad\text{at every smooth point of }\gamma,\tag{1} \]

then each part of the interface either moves into the side of \(q\) or slides along itself, and \(q\) can be slid a little in the direction \(w\) (Theorem 2 below makes this precise).1

Condition (1) says that all normals of the interface lie in the closed half-plane \(\{v\colon v\cdot w\ge0\}\). Hence, to block every slide, the normals must lie in no closed half-plane: marked on the unit circle, their directions must not fit in any closed half-circle.

Figure 2: sliding

Pull \(q\) in some direction \(w\); it slides along a straight line until it hits \(p\). Arrows: some of the normals \(n\). Right: the directions of all the normals, marked on the unit circle; the green arc, when there is one, consists of the directions \(w\) satisfying (1). Normals with \(n\cdot w\lt0\) are drawn in red.

Turning

Next, turn \(q\) counterclockwise about a pivot \(z\). A point \(x\) then moves with velocity \((x-z)^\perp\). Since rotating two vectors by \(90^\circ\) does not change their inner product, the normal component of this velocity along the interface is

\[ n\cdot(\gamma-z)^\perp=\dot\gamma^\perp\cdot(\gamma-z)^\perp=\dot\gamma\cdot(\gamma-z)=\frac12\,\frac{d}{dt}\,|\gamma-z|^2 .\tag{2} \]

So the analogue of (1) for counterclockwise turns about \(z\) is that the distance \(|\gamma(t)-z|\) is nondecreasing in \(t\); for clockwise turns, that it is nonincreasing. For example, if the interface is an arc of a spiral around \(z\), then \(q\) can be unscrewed. To block every turn, the interface must therefore, viewed from every point \(z\) of the plane, somewhere recede from \(z\) and somewhere approach it.

As \(z\) recedes to infinity, a turn about \(z\) looks more and more like a slide, and the condition for turns becomes (1).

Figure 3: turning

Pick up the pin to choose a pivot \(z\), then pull \(q\) to turn it about \(z\). While you hold the pin, the interface is drawn blue where it recedes from \(z\) and orange where it approaches \(z\). The green region consists of the pivots from which the interface only recedes or only approaches, i.e. the pivots about which \(q\) can be turned. By (2), for each point of the interface the condition is linear in \(z\), so this region is the union of two convex sets.

Wiggling does not help

The cleanest setting is an idealized puzzle: the whole plane, cut into two pieces along a single curve which goes off to infinity at both ends. Here turns are blocked automatically. Indeed, \(|\gamma(t)-z|\to\infty\) as \(t\to\pm\infty\), so this distance is monotone for no \(z\). Only slides remain, and they decide everything:

Theorem 1 (an infinite cut)

Let the plane be cut into two pieces \(p,q\) along a piecewise-smooth embedded curve \(\gamma\) going to infinity at both ends.

  1. If (1) holds for some \(w\neq0\), then \(q+sw\) does not overlap \(p\) for any \(s\ge0\): the piece \(q\) slides away to infinity.
  2. Otherwise, \(p\) and \(q\) are interlocked.

In particular, a round head on a straight line interlocks two half-planes, even without a neck: the normals along the head sweep more than a half-circle of directions. In a bounded puzzle, the same head can be turned out of its socket (try the third puzzle of Figure 1 in the turn mode, with the pin at the center of the head).

Figure 4: the whole plane, cut in two

A window onto an idealized puzzle: the cut continues to infinity in both directions. Try every mode; no turn about any pin moves \(q\), since the far ends of the cut are in the way.

A real puzzle, however, is bounded. This has two effects. First, turns become genuinely available, and must be blocked by the shape of the interface. Second, the interface has endpoints, near which one piece may obstruct the other in ways that the normals of the interface do not detect. To rule this out, we require the pieces to be in clean contact: roughly speaking, near every point of the interface the two pieces lie on opposite sides of it, also near its endpoints, where the interface is continued by its tangent rays. This holds, for instance, when a board with smooth boundary is cut into two pieces along a curve meeting the boundary transversally.

Precise definition of clean contact

The pieces \(p,q\) are in clean contact if every point of \(\gamma\) other than an endpoint has a neighborhood \(U\) with \(U\setminus\gamma\subseteq p\cup q\), and the following holds at each endpoint \(x_0\) of \(\gamma\) (if any). Let \(\hat\gamma\) be the curve obtained by continuing \(\gamma\) beyond \(x_0\) by its tangent ray at \(x_0\). Then in some neighborhood \(U\) of \(x_0\), the curve \(\hat\gamma\) separates \(U\) into two components, one containing \(p\cap U\) and the other containing \(q\cap U\), and no boundary arc of \(p\) or of \(q\) other than \(\gamma\) is tangent to \(\hat\gamma\) at \(x_0\).

Theorem 2 (a bounded puzzle)

Let \(p,q\) be adjacent pieces of a jigsaw puzzle in clean contact. Then exactly one of the following holds.

  1. A single small slide or a single small turn moves \(q\) without overlapping \(p\).
  2. The pieces are interlocked.

The second alternative occurs if and only if

  1. the normals of \(\gamma\) lie in no closed half-plane, and
  2. for every \(z\in\mathbb{R}^2\), the distance \(|\gamma(t)-z|\) is strictly increasing somewhere and strictly decreasing somewhere.

In Figure 3, condition (b) says that the green region is empty. The standard jigsaw tab satisfies (a) because of its head, as above. It satisfies (b) because of its neck: from the center of the head, the head is at constant distance, but the interface recedes along one wall of the neck and approaches along the other; from any other pivot, the head itself both recedes and approaches.

On the proofs. The idea is to look at the initial velocity of an admissible motion. Every rigid motion close to the identity is, to first order, an infinitesimal slide \(V(x)=b\) or an infinitesimal turn \(V(x)=\omega(x-z)^\perp\) about some pivot \(z\). If a motion starting with velocity \(V\) is admissible, then \(V\) cannot push \(q\) into \(p\) anywhere, i.e. \(n\cdot V\ge0\) along \(\gamma\). By (2), this means that (1) holds for \(w=b\), or that \(|\gamma-z|\) is monotone. Thus (a) and (b) block every admissible motion; this direction does not use clean contact. Conversely, if (1) holds, a barrier argument shows that the slide in the direction \(w\) actually works for a short time, and similarly for turns. Clean contact enters here, near the endpoints of the interface.

Proof that an admissible motion has an admissible initial velocity

Let \(g(t)\), \(0\le t\le1\), be an admissible motion with \(g(0)=\mathrm{id}\). We may assume that \(g(t)\neq\mathrm{id}\) for arbitrarily small \(t\gt0\), for otherwise we start the motion at the last time at which \(g=\mathrm{id}\). Choose \(t_j\searrow0\) with \(g(t_j)\neq\mathrm{id}\). For large \(j\), \(g(t_j)\) is the rotation by a small angle \(\theta_j\) followed by the translation by a small vector \(a_j\). Put \(r_j=(|a_j|^2+\theta_j^2)^{1/2}\) and pass to a subsequence such that \(a_j/r_j\to b\) and \(\theta_j/r_j\to\omega\), so that \((b,\omega)\neq(0,0)\). Then

\[ g(t_j)(y)=y+r_jV(y)+o(r_j),\qquad V(y):=b+\omega y^\perp, \]

uniformly for \(y\) in compact sets. If \(\omega\neq0\), then \(V(y)=\omega(y-z)^\perp\) with \(z=b^\perp/\omega\).

Suppose that \(n\cdot V(x)\lt0\) at some smooth point \(x\) of \(\gamma\), and choose \(\alpha\gt0\) with \(\alpha+n\cdot V(x)\lt0\). Near \(x\), let \(f\) be the signed distance to \(\gamma\), positive on the side of \(q\), so that \(\nabla f(x)=n\). For large \(j\), the point \(x_j=x+\alpha r_jn\) lies in \(q\), and

\[ f\big(g(t_j)(x_j)\big)=r_j\big(\alpha+n\cdot V(x)\big)+o(r_j)\lt0, \]

i.e. \(g(t_j)(x_j)\in p\), contradicting admissibility.

Why (1) lets \(q\) slide

Near a smooth point of \(\gamma\), let \(f\) be the signed distance to \(\gamma\), positive on the side of \(q\). The function \(\nabla f\cdot w\) is Lipschitz and, by (1), nonnegative on \(\gamma=\{f=0\}\); hence \(\nabla f\cdot w\ge-Cf\) on the side of \(q\) for some constant \(C\). If the segment \(s\mapsto y+sw\) starts at a point \(y\) of \(q\) near \(\gamma\), then \(\varphi(s)=f(y+sw)\) satisfies \(\varphi'\ge-C\varphi\) as long as \(\varphi\gt0\), whence \(\varphi(s)\ge\varphi(0)e^{-Cs}\gt0\). Thus the segment never reaches \(\gamma\), and \(\gamma\) acts as a one-way barrier.

Corners of \(\gamma\) are avoided by perturbing the starting point. For an infinite cut this already shows that \(q+sw\) and \(p\) are disjoint for all \(s\ge0\). For a bounded puzzle, a compactness argument gives a uniform time, and near the endpoints of \(\gamma\) the same argument is run with the continued curve \(\hat\gamma\) from the definition of clean contact; the analysis there is more delicate and is omitted. Turns are handled in the same way, with segments replaced by circular arcs about \(z\).

Readers from robotics will recognize the first-order part of Theorem 2. Encode the contact at a point \(x\) of the interface by the vector \((n,\ x\wedge n)\in\mathbb{R}^3\), where \(x\wedge n=x_1n_2-x_2n_1\). Conditions (a) and (b) together say that these vectors are not contained in any closed half-space of \(\mathbb{R}^3\). This is the planar case of form closure in the theory of grasping and fixturing2, which goes back to Reuleaux3. What Theorem 2 adds is the passage from the first-order test to arbitrary motions. This passage is not automatic: a body which the first-order test declares free may still be immobilized by second-order, curvature effects4, and conversely, as the next section shows.

… unless the contact is not clean

Clean contact cannot be dropped from Theorem 2. In the first example, \(q\) is blocked by material of \(p\) which contributes no normals to the interface.

The sliver. Cut the unit disk into three sectors: \(q\) between the angles \(-60^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\), a thin sliver between \(-70^\circ\) and \(-60^\circ\), and \(p\) between \(90^\circ\) and \(290^\circ\). The interface of \(p\) and \(q\) is the vertical radius, and its only normal is \(n=(1,0)\). The downward direction \(w=(0,-1)\) satisfies (1), yet \(q\) cannot slide downward even a little: near the center, \(p\) wraps around the lower endpoint of the interface, and points of \(q\) near the center immediately enter \(p\). (The pieces are not interlocked: \(q\) can be slid to the right.)

Figure 5: the sliver

Pull \(q\) straight down, then to the right. The sliver is in light grey.

In the second example the contact fails to be clean in a subtler, tangential way, and wiggling genuinely helps.

The drilled channel. Drill a channel of width \(2r\) along a parabolic arc \(\Gamma\), from a point \(E\) inside a board to its edge, and let \(q\) be the disk of radius \(r\) resting at the closed end of the channel, centered at \(E\). The interface is the semicircular end cap of the channel; the walls of the channel leave its endpoints tangentially, so the contact is not clean. Whatever the motion, the center of the disk must stay on \(\Gamma\) (a disk of radius \(r\) fits in the channel only when centered on \(\Gamma\)). A slide or a turn moves the center along a straight segment or a circular arc, and a parabola contains neither. So no single slide or turn moves \(q\) (except the spin about \(E\), which does not move the disk at all), and yet \(q\) slides out along the channel.

Figure 6: the drilled channel

In “wiggle” mode, pull the ball out along the channel. Then try a single straight slide, or a single turn about the pin, which starts at the center of the circle that best approximates \(\Gamma\) at \(E\). (The ball fits its slot with a hair of play, so it can move a tiny bit in these modes.)

We do not know whether clean contact is also needed for the other direction of Theorem 2, i.e. whether two pieces can be interlocked although (a) or (b) fails. A natural candidate is a round tab held in a socket which it touches only along an arc at the bottom, while the lips of the socket hug the neck across two thin tapering gaps. The first-order test then permits the turn about the center of the head, and the question is whether the tapering gaps nevertheless block it. This is a second-order question of the kind studied by Rimon and Burdick4.

How much must the cut bend?

The total curvature of \(\gamma\) is the total angle through which its tangent turns: \(\int|\kappa|\,dt\), plus the sum of the absolute exterior angles at the corners.

Proposition

If two pieces are interlocked (across an infinite cut, or in a bounded puzzle with clean contact), then the total curvature of their interface exceeds \(\pi\). The constant \(\pi\) is sharp.

Indeed, if the total curvature is at most \(\pi\), then the angle of the tangent \(\dot\gamma\), and hence that of the normal \(n\), takes values in an interval of length at most \(\pi\). Thus the normals lie in a closed half-plane, and by Theorem 1 or Theorem 2 a slide moves \(q\).

Sharpness is shown by the hook of Figure 7: a channel of width \(2\cos\frac\delta2\) ending in a round head of radius \(1\). The interface consists of two straight walls and a circular arc of angular width \(\pi+\delta\), meeting at two corners of exterior angle \(\delta/2\), so its total curvature is \(\pi+2\delta\). Along the arc the normals point at its center, so they sweep an arc of directions of length \(\pi+\delta\), and (a) holds. Condition (b) holds because the two walls are traversed in opposite directions. Viewed from a pivot \(z\) to the right of the left edge of the board, the interface approaches \(z\) near the left end of the lower wall and recedes from it near the left end of the upper wall; viewed from any other pivot, it is the other way round near the right ends of the walls. Hence the hook interlocks the two pieces, both across an infinite cut and in a bounded board, and letting \(\delta\to0\) shows that \(\pi\) is sharp.

The extremal shapes differ from those in the theorems of Fenchel5 and of Fáry and Milnor6, where the total curvature of a closed curve is at least \(2\pi\), with equality only for plane convex curves. Here a circular arc alone does not suffice in a bounded board: (b) fails at its center, and the piece can be turned out of its socket (try the third puzzle of Figure 1 in the turn mode, with the pin at the center of the head). The straight walls of the neck cost almost no bending, and block that turn.

Figure 7: the hook

The slider changes \(\delta\). With “show the math” on, the slide and turn modes display the normals and the colored interface of Figures 2 and 3.

For a closed interface, such as an island \(q\) fitted into a hole of \(p\), the threshold is \(2\pi\), and it is attained. Here (a) always holds: if \(\dot\gamma\cdot w\ge0\) everywhere, then \(\oint\dot\gamma\,dt=0\) forces \(\dot\gamma\cdot w\equiv0\), which is impossible for a closed curve. And if \(\dot\gamma\cdot(\gamma-z)\) never changes sign, then \(|\gamma-z|^2\) is monotone and periodic, hence constant, so \(\gamma\) is a circle about \(z\). Thus an island is interlocked unless it is a disk, which can spin in its hole. By Fenchel's theorem, every closed curve has total curvature at least \(2\pi\), with equality for every convex island.
