Why jigsaw pieces cannot slide apart

Rotem Assouline, Itai Benjamini and Claude

Last updated: October 31, 2026

The following question arose when one of us (Itai) was assembling a two-piece jigsaw puzzle with his toddler granddaughter Ayala. To separate two adjacent pieces of a jigsaw puzzle, one has to lift one of them off the table: no amount of sliding and turning in the plane will do. Which geometric property of the cut between the two pieces is responsible for this?

The question

A jigsaw puzzle is a board cut into pieces. Two pieces \(p\) and \(q\) are adjacent if they touch, and the curve along which they touch is their interface, which we denote by \(\gamma\).

Formally

The board is a compact subset of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) containing finitely many pieces. The pieces are pairwise disjoint connected open sets, each bounded by finitely many piecewise-smooth simple closed curves. Two pieces \(p\) and \(q\) are adjacent if \(\overline p\cap\overline q\neq\varnothing\). We assume that their interface \(\gamma:=\overline p\cap\overline q\) is a piecewise-smooth simple curve, either an arc or a closed curve. This definition does not cover the unbounded puzzle of Theorem 1, whose two pieces are described in its statement.

A board cut into six pieces. The pieces \(p\) and \(q\) are adjacent, and their interface \(\gamma\) is drawn thick.

We travel along the interface with \(q\) on our left. At each point of the interface, the normal \(n\) is an arrow which is perpendicular to the interface and points into the piece \(q\).

Formally

We parametrize \(\gamma\) by arclength, oriented so that \(q\) lies to its left. At every point where \(\gamma\) is smooth, \(\dot\gamma\) denotes the unit tangent and \(n\) the unit normal pointing into \(q\). Thus \(n=\dot\gamma^\perp\), where \(v^\perp\) denotes the vector \(v\) rotated by \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise.

The interface \(\gamma\), traversed with \(q\) on its left, and the unit tangent \(\dot\gamma\) and unit normal \(n\) at one of its points.

We hold \(p\) in place and try to move the piece \(q\) in the plane, without lifting it. The two pieces must not overlap. (They may, however, stay in contact, so \(q\) is allowed to slide along \(p\).) Such a motion is admissible. If no admissible motion moves \(q\) at all, the pieces are interlocked.

Formally

A motion of \(q\) is a continuous family \(g_\tau\), \(0\le\tau\le1\), of orientation-preserving isometries of \(\mathbb{R}^2\), with \(g_0\) being the identity. It is admissible if \(g_\tau(q)\cap p=\varnothing\) for every \(\tau\). Since the pieces are open sets, \(\overline{g_\tau(q)}\cap\overline p\) may be nonempty: the pieces may stay in contact. The pieces are interlocked if for every admissible motion, \(g_\tau\) is the identity for all \(\tau\).

Two kinds of motions are easy to test: sliding \(q\) in a fixed direction, and turning it about a fixed pivot. We first find the conditions under which these fail, and then show that, under a mild assumption, they are the only motions that need to be tested.

A slide of \(q\) in the direction of a vector \(w\), and a turn of \(q\) about a pivot \(z\).

Exercises
  1. Interlocking is symmetric. Show that \(p\) and \(q\) are interlocked if and only if no admissible motion moves \(p\) while \(q\) is held in place.
    Solution

    Let \(g_\tau\), \(0\le\tau\le1\), be a motion of \(q\), and put \(h_\tau=g_\tau^{-1}\). Then \(h\) is again a continuous family of orientation-preserving isometries, \(h_0\) is the identity, and \(h_\tau\) is the identity exactly when \(g_\tau\) is. Since \(g_\tau\) is a bijection, \(g_\tau(q)\cap p=\varnothing\) if and only if \(q\cap h_\tau(p)=\varnothing\). So \(g\) is an admissible motion of \(q\) that moves it if and only if \(h\) is an admissible motion of \(p\) that moves it.

  2. Why do we allow the pieces to stay in contact? Find a puzzle in which \(q\) can be slid out, but in which \(\overline{g_\tau(q)}\cap\overline p\neq\varnothing\) for every motion \(g\) that moves \(q\) a little, and for all small \(\tau\).
    Solution

    Cut a rectangular slot into the board, open at the edge of the board, and let \(q\) be a rectangle filling it, so that \(q\) touches \(p\) along three of its sides. Sliding \(q\) along the slot moves it out of the board without overlap, but the long sides of \(q\) stay in contact with the walls of the slot until \(q\) is out. Any motion that moves \(q\) a little must keep \(q\) in the slot, as a rectangle of the slot's width fits in the slot only when it is parallel to the walls; so the closures of the pieces meet at all small times. If contact were forbidden, \(q\) could not be moved at all, although it can be taken out by a slide.

Sliding

Suppose we slide the piece \(q\) in some direction. Wherever a normal of the interface makes an obtuse angle with the direction of the slide, the slide pushes \(q\) into \(p\) at once. If, on the other hand, no normal makes an obtuse angle with it, then each part of the interface either moves into the side of \(q\) or slides along itself, and \(q\) can be slid a little in that direction.1,2 Hence, in order for it to be impossible to separate the pieces by a slide, the normals must point in sufficiently many directions: if we place all normals on a single circle, they should not all fit in a closed half-circle.

Formally

Let \(w\neq0\) be the direction of the slide. At a smooth point of \(\gamma\) where \(n\cdot w\lt 0\), the slide immediately pushes \(q\) into \(p\). If, on the other hand,

\[ n\cdot w\ge 0\qquad\text{at every smooth point of }\gamma,\tag{1} \]

then, under the assumption of clean contact, \(q+sw\) does not meet \(p\) for small \(s\gt0\) (Theorem 2 below makes this precise). Condition (1) says that all normals of \(\gamma\) lie in the closed half-plane \(\{v\colon v\cdot w\ge0\}\), which is bounded by a line through the origin. Hence (1) fails for every \(w\neq0\) if and only if the normals lie in no closed half-plane bounded by a line through the origin.

Exercises
  1. Let \(N\) be the set of normals of the interface, and let \(K\) be its convex hull. Show that no \(w\neq0\) satisfies (1) if and only if the origin is an interior point of \(K\).
    Solution

    If \(N\subseteq\{v\colon v\cdot w\ge0\}\) for some \(w\neq0\), then \(K\) lies in the same half-plane, and the origin, which lies on its boundary line, is not an interior point of \(K\). Conversely, if the origin is not an interior point of the convex set \(K\), then there is a line through the origin with \(K\) on one side of it (by the separation theorem if \(0\notin\overline K\), and by the supporting line theorem if \(0\) lies on the boundary of \(K\)). That is, \(K\subseteq\{v\colon v\cdot w\ge0\}\) for some \(w\neq0\), and so is \(N\).

  2. In the dovetail of Figure 2, the interface consists of a horizontal segment, a segment from \((0.6,-1)\) to \((0,0)\), and another horizontal segment. Find all directions in which \(q\) can slide.
    Solution

    Along the horizontal segments, \(n=(0,1)\), and along the slanted segment, \(n=(-1,-0.6)/|(-1,-0.6)|\). A direction \(w\) satisfies (1) if and only if \(w_2\ge0\) and \(w_1+0.6\,w_2\le0\). These are the directions between \((-1,0)\), straight to the left, and \((-0.6,1)\), parallel to the slanted segment; in Figure 2, this is the green arc.

Turning

Next, we try to turn the piece \(q\) about a pivot \(z\). Travelling along the interface with \(q\) on our left, we find that \(q\) can be turned counterclockwise about \(z\) if the interface never approaches \(z\), and clockwise if it never recedes from \(z\). For example, if the interface is an arc of a spiral around \(z\), then \(q\) can be unscrewed. To block every turn, the interface must, viewed from every point \(z\) of the plane, somewhere recede from \(z\) and somewhere approach it. As \(z\) moves off to infinity, a turn about \(z\) looks more and more like a slide.

Formally

Under the counterclockwise turn about \(z\), a point \(x\) moves with velocity \((x-z)^\perp\). Since \(n=\dot\gamma^\perp\), and rotating two vectors by \(90^\circ\) does not change their inner product, the normal component of the velocity along the interface is

\[ n\cdot(\gamma-z)^\perp=\dot\gamma^\perp\cdot(\gamma-z)^\perp=\dot\gamma\cdot(\gamma-z)=\frac12\,\frac{d}{dt}\,|\gamma-z|^2 .\tag{2} \]

So the analogue of (1) for counterclockwise turns about \(z\) is that \(t\mapsto|\gamma(t)-z|\) is nondecreasing, and for clockwise turns, that it is nonincreasing. As \(z\) goes to infinity in a fixed direction \(u\), the normalized velocity field \((x-z)^\perp/|z|\) converges to the constant vector \(w=-u^\perp\), and the condition for turns becomes (1).

Exercises
  1. Show that the set of pivots \(z\) with \(\dot\gamma\cdot(\gamma-z)\ge0\) at every smooth point of \(\gamma\) is convex, and similarly for \(\dot\gamma\cdot(\gamma-z)\le0\).
    Solution

    For a fixed smooth point \(\gamma(t)\), the condition \(\dot\gamma(t)\cdot(\gamma(t)-z)\ge0\) reads \(z\cdot\dot\gamma(t)\le\gamma(t)\cdot\dot\gamma(t)\). Since \(\dot\gamma(t)\neq0\), this is a closed half-plane of pivots \(z\). The set in question is the intersection of these half-planes over all smooth points, and an intersection of convex sets is convex. For clockwise turns, the inequalities are reversed.

  2. Show that \(\dot\gamma\cdot(\gamma-z)=0\) at every smooth point of \(\gamma\) if and only if \(\gamma\) is an arc of a circle centered at \(z\), or the whole circle.
    Solution

    By (2), \(\dot\gamma\cdot(\gamma-z)=0\) at every smooth point if and only if the derivative of \(|\gamma(t)-z|^2\) vanishes away from the corners. As this function is continuous, this happens if and only if \(|\gamma(t)-z|\) is constant, i.e. \(\gamma\) lies on a circle centered at \(z\).

Wiggling does not help

The cleanest setting is an idealized puzzle: the whole plane, cut into two pieces along a single curve which goes off to infinity at both ends. Here turns are blocked automatically: whatever the pivot, the interface comes in from infinity and goes back out to infinity, so it somewhere approaches the pivot and somewhere recedes from it. Slides are then the only way to separate the pieces.

Formally

Parametrize \(\gamma\) by arclength, \(\gamma\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^2\). That both ends go to infinity means that \(|\gamma(t)|\to\infty\) as \(t\to\pm\infty\). Fix \(z\in\mathbb{R}^2\) and put \(f(t)=|\gamma(t)-z|^2\). The function \(f\) is piecewise smooth, with \(f'=2\,\dot\gamma\cdot(\gamma-z)\) away from the corners, and \(f(t)\to\infty\) as \(t\to\pm\infty\). If \(\dot\gamma\cdot(\gamma-z)\ge0\) at every smooth point, then \(f\) is nondecreasing, so \(f(t)\le f(0)\) for all \(t\le0\), contradicting \(f(t)\to\infty\) as \(t\to-\infty\). Hence \(\dot\gamma\cdot(\gamma-z)\lt0\) at some smooth point, and likewise, using \(t\to+\infty\), \(\dot\gamma\cdot(\gamma-z)\gt0\) at another. By (2), a counterclockwise turn about \(z\) pushes \(q\) into \(p\) at the first point, and a clockwise turn at the second.

Theorem 1

Let \(\gamma\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^2\) be an injective, piecewise-smooth curve, parametrized by arclength, with \(|\gamma(t)|\to\infty\) as \(t\to\pm\infty\). Then \(\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\gamma\) has two components; let \(q\) be the component lying to the left of \(\gamma\) and denote the other component by \(p\). Let \(n\) be the unit normal of \(\gamma\) pointing into \(q\).

  1. If there is a vector \(w\neq0\) with \(n\cdot w\ge0\) at every smooth point of \(\gamma\), then \(q\) can be slid away to infinity in the direction \(w\): \((q+sw)\cap p=\varnothing\) for every \(s\ge0\).
  2. Otherwise, \(p\) and \(q\) are interlocked.
Proof of Theorem 1

  1. Rotating the picture, we may assume that \(w=(0,1)\). For a point \(x \in \mathbb{R}^2\), write \(x=(x_1,x_2)\), and similarly \(\gamma=(\gamma_1,\gamma_2)\). Since \(n=\dot\gamma^\perp\) and \(v^\perp=(-v_2,v_1)\) for every \(v\in\mathbb{R}^2\), we have \(n=(-\dot\gamma_2,\dot\gamma_1)\). Thus the assumption \(n\cdot w\ge0\) reads

    \[ n\cdot w=\dot\gamma_1\ge0\qquad\text{at every smooth point of }\gamma, \]

    Since \(\gamma_1\) is continuous and piecewise smooth, this means that \(\gamma_1\) is nondecreasing.

    Assume by contradiction that there exist \(y\in q\) and \(s\gt0\) such that \(y+sw\in p\). The vertical line through \(y\) may contain a segment of \(\gamma\), along which \(\dot\gamma_1=0\). We therefore first move \(y\) sideways to a vertical line through a point \(\gamma(t_0)\) with \(\dot\gamma_1(t_0)\gt0\) (see the figure below). Since \(p\) and \(q\) are open, there is \(\delta\gt0\) such that

    \[ y+(u,0)\in q\qquad\text{and}\qquad y+(u,0)+sw\in p\qquad\text{for every }u\in(-\delta,\delta). \]

    Each vertical segment from \(y+(u,0)\) to \(y+(u,0)+sw\) joins \(q\) to \(p\), so it meets \(\gamma\). Hence \(\gamma_1\) takes the values \(y_1-\delta/2\) and \(y_1+\delta/2\), at times \(t_-\lt t_+\), say. Since \(\gamma_1\) is piecewise smooth and not constant on \([t_-,t_+]\), there is a smooth point \(t_0\in(t_-,t_+)\) with \(\dot\gamma_1(t_0)\neq0\), i.e. \(\dot\gamma_1(t_0)\gt0\). Since \(\gamma_1\) is nondecreasing, \(|\gamma_1(t_0)-y_1|\le\delta/2\), so the point \(y'=y+\big(\gamma_1(t_0)-y_1,0\big)\) satisfies \(y'\in q\) and \(y'+sw\in p\). Both points lie on the vertical line \(L\) through \(\gamma(t_0)\).

    The vertical segments from \(y+(u,0)\) to \(y+(u,0)+sw\), \(|u|\lt\delta\), all meet \(\gamma\). The segment through \(y\) may run along \(\gamma\), but the segment through \(y'\) meets \(\gamma\) only at \(\gamma(t_0)\).

    Since \(\gamma_1\) is nondecreasing and \(\dot\gamma_1(t_0)\gt0\),

    \[ \gamma_1(t)\lt\gamma_1(t_0)\quad\text{for }t\lt t_0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\gamma_1(t)\gt\gamma_1(t_0)\quad\text{for }t\gt t_0, \]

    so \(L\) meets \(\gamma\) only at \(\gamma(t_0)\). Hence each of the two open half-lines into which \(\gamma(t_0)\) divides \(L\) is a connected subset of \(p\cup q\), and therefore lies in \(p\) or in \(q\). Since \(n\cdot w=\dot\gamma_1(t_0)\gt0\), the points \(\gamma(t_0)+\varepsilon w\) lie in \(q\), and the points \(\gamma(t_0)-\varepsilon w\) lie in \(p\), for small \(\varepsilon\gt0\). Thus the points of \(L\) above \(\gamma(t_0)\) lie in \(q\), and the points of \(L\) below \(\gamma(t_0)\) lie in \(p\). In particular, \(y'\in q\) lies above \(\gamma(t_0)\). Hence so does \(y'+sw\), which therefore lies in \(q\), a contradiction.

  2. Suppose that no \(w\neq0\) satisfies \(n\cdot w\ge0\) at every smooth point of \(\gamma\), and let \(g_\tau\), \(0\le\tau\le1\), be an admissible motion which is not constant. We may assume that \(g_\tau\) is not the identity for arbitrarily small \(\tau\gt0\), for otherwise we start the motion at the last time at which \(g_\tau\) is the identity. Choose \(\tau_j\searrow0\) such that \(g_{\tau_j}\) is not the identity. Every orientation-preserving isometry is a rotation about the origin followed by a translation,3 so

    \[ g_{\tau_j}(y)=(\cos\theta_j)\,y+(\sin\theta_j)\,y^\perp+a_j,\qquad\text{where }\theta_j\in(-\pi,\pi]\text{ and }a_j\in\mathbb{R}^2. \]

    Since \(g\) is continuous and \(g_0\) is the identity, \(\theta_j\to0\) and \(a_j\to0\). Put

    \[ r_j=\big(|a_j|^2+\theta_j^2\big)^{1/2}, \]

    and pass to a subsequence such that \(a_j/r_j\to b\) and \(\theta_j/r_j\to\omega\), so that \((b,\omega)\neq(0,0)\). Then

    \[ g_{\tau_j}(y)=y+r_jV(y)+o(r_j),\qquad\text{where }V(y)=b+\omega y^\perp, \]

    uniformly for \(y\) in compact sets. We claim that there is a smooth point \(x\) of \(\gamma\) such that

    \[ n\cdot V(x)\lt0.\tag{3} \]

    The vector field \(V\): a translation if \(\omega=0\), and a rotation about \(z=b^\perp/\omega\) if \(\omega\neq0\).

    If \(\omega=0\), then \(V=b\neq0\), and the claim follows from the assumption that no \(w\neq0\) satisfies \(n\cdot w\ge0\) at every smooth point. If \(\omega\neq0\), then \(V(y)=\omega(y-z)^\perp\), where \(z=b^\perp/\omega\), and by (2),

    \[ n\cdot V(\gamma)=\omega\,\dot\gamma\cdot(\gamma-z)=\frac\omega2\,f',\qquad\text{where }f(t)=|\gamma(t)-z|^2. \]

    The function \(f\) is continuous, and \(f(t)\to\infty\) as \(t\to\pm\infty\), since \(|\gamma(t)|\to\infty\). If \(\omega f'\ge0\) at every smooth point, then \(f\) is nondecreasing if \(\omega\gt0\), so that \(f(t)\le f(0)\) for all \(t\le0\), and nonincreasing if \(\omega\lt0\), so that \(f(t)\le f(0)\) for all \(t\ge0\). Both contradict \(f(t)\to\infty\), as \(t\to-\infty\) and as \(t\to+\infty\), respectively, which proves (3).

    The vector \(V\) at points of \(\gamma\). Left: \(\omega=0\); the normals of \(\gamma\) are not contained in a half-plane. Right: \(\omega\gt0\).

    Choose \(\alpha\gt0\) such that the vector \(e=\alpha n+V(x)\) satisfies

    \[ e\cdot n=\alpha+n\cdot V(x)\lt0, \]

    and choose \(\theta\in(0,\pi/2)\) with \(e\cdot n\lt-|e|\sin\theta\). For \(\rho\gt0\), consider the sectors

    \[ S_\pm=\{x+v\colon 0\lt|v|\lt\rho,\ \pm v\cdot n\gt|v|\sin\theta\}, \]

    which consist of the points near \(x\) seen from \(x\) at an angle greater than \(\theta\) from the tangent line. Write \(x=\gamma(t)\). Since

    \[ \gamma(t+\sigma)=x+\sigma\dot\gamma(t)+o(\sigma)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\dot\gamma(t)\cdot n=0, \]

    there is \(\sigma_0\gt0\) such that \(\gamma(t+\sigma)\) lies in neither sector for \(0\lt|\sigma|\lt\sigma_0\). Since \(\gamma\) is injective and \(|\gamma|\to\infty\) at both ends, the points \(\gamma(t+\sigma)\) with \(|\sigma|\ge\sigma_0\) stay away from \(x\). Hence, for small \(\rho\), neither sector meets \(\gamma\). Each sector is connected, so it lies in \(p\) or in \(q\), and since \(n\) points into \(q\),

    \[ S_+\subseteq q\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_-\subseteq p. \]

    Now put \(x_j=x+\alpha r_jn\). Then \(x_j\in S_+\subseteq q\) for large \(j\), and

    \[ g_{\tau_j}(x_j)=x+r_je+o(r_j). \]

    Since \(e\cdot n\lt-|e|\sin\theta\), it follows that \(g_{\tau_j}(x_j)\in S_-\subseteq p\) for large \(j\). Thus \(g_{\tau_j}(q)\) meets \(p\), which contradicts the assumption that \(g\) is admissible.

    The sectors \(S_+\subseteq q\) and \(S_-\subseteq p\) at \(x\). The motion \(g_{\tau_j}\) carries \(x_j\in S_+\) into \(S_-\).

Exercises
  1. Cut the plane along a straight line carrying a round head whose arc is longer than a half-circle, as below. Show that the two pieces are interlocked.
    Solution

    Along the arc, the normal at each point is the direction from that point to the center of the arc, so these normals cover an arc of directions longer than a half-circle. A closed half-plane through the origin contains only a closed half-circle of directions, so it cannot contain all of them. By Theorem 1, the pieces are interlocked.

… unless the contact is not clean

A real puzzle, however, is bounded. This has two effects. First, turns become genuinely available, and must be excluded by the shape of the interface. Second, the interface has endpoints, near which one piece may obstruct the other in ways that the normals of the interface do not detect. To rule this out, we require the pieces to be in clean contact: roughly speaking, near every point of the interface the two pieces lie on opposite sides of it, and near an endpoint of the interface they lie on opposite sides of its continuation.

For instance, clean contact holds when a board with smooth boundary is cut into two pieces along a curve meeting the boundary transversally.

Precise definition of clean contact

The pieces \(p,q\) are in clean contact if the following two conditions hold.

  • Every point of \(\gamma\) other than an endpoint has a neighborhood \(U\) with \(U\setminus\gamma\subseteq p\cup q\).
  • Let \(x_0\) be an endpoint of \(\gamma\), and let \(\hat\gamma\) be the curve obtained by continuing \(\gamma\) beyond \(x_0\) by its tangent ray at \(x_0\), so that \(\hat\gamma\) is affine away from \(\gamma\). Then in some neighborhood \(U\) of \(x_0\), the curve \(\hat\gamma\) separates \(U\) into two components, one containing \(p\cap U\) and the other containing \(q\cap U\), and no boundary arc of \(p\) or of \(q\) other than \(\gamma\) is tangent to \(\hat\gamma\) at \(x_0\).
Theorem 2

Let \(p,q\) be adjacent pieces of a jigsaw puzzle in clean contact. Parametrize their interface \(\gamma\) by arclength, so that \(q\) lies to its left, and let \(n\) be the unit normal of \(\gamma\) pointing into \(q\). For \(z\in\mathbb{R}^2\) and \(\theta\in\mathbb{R}\), let \(\rho_z(\theta)\) be the counterclockwise rotation about \(z\) by the angle \(\theta\).

  1. If a vector \(w\neq0\) satisfies \(n\cdot w\ge0\) at every smooth point of \(\gamma\), then \(q\) can be moved by a slide in the direction \(w\): there is \(s_0\gt0\) with \((q+sw)\cap p=\varnothing\) for every \(s\in[0,s_0]\).
  2. If a point \(z\in\mathbb{R}^2\) and a sign \(\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}\) satisfy \(\varepsilon\,\dot\gamma\cdot(\gamma-z)\ge0\) at every smooth point of \(\gamma\), then \(q\) can be moved by a turn about \(z\): there is \(\theta_0\gt0\) with \(\rho_z(\varepsilon\theta)(q)\cap p=\varnothing\) for every \(\theta\in[0,\theta_0]\).
  3. Otherwise, \(p\) and \(q\) are interlocked.

In particular, if \(q\) can be moved at all, then it can already be moved by a single slide or a single turn. The proof of Theorem 2 is given in the appendix.

Clean contact cannot be dropped from Theorem 2, as the next two examples show.

The wedged tooth. Take two triangles with the same horizontal base \(AB\) and base angles less than \(60^\circ\), the second slightly less tall than the first, and a horizontal line which crosses both of them. Let \(p\) be the part of a board below the line, with the taller triangle cut out of it, and let \(q\) be the part above the line, raised slightly, together with the part of the shorter triangle below the line, which forms a tooth. Then \(q\) rests on \(p\) along \(AB\), with its tooth wedged in the triangular slot of \(p\), and elsewhere a narrow gap separates the pieces. So the interface is the segment \(AB\), and all its normals point straight up. Thus, without the assumption of clean contact, statement of Theorem 2 would predict that \(q\) can be slid upward. In fact \(q\) cannot move at all.

The drilled channel. Drill a winding channel from a point inside a board out to its edge, and let \(q\) be a disk which fits the channel exactly, resting at its closed end. The disk touches \(p\) only along the rounded end of the channel, and the walls of the channel leave this interface tangentially, so the contact is not clean. To stay inside the channel, the disk must follow its curve.

Formally

Let \(\Gamma\) be a wavy arc, such as an arc of the graph of a cosine, from a point \(E\) inside the board to its edge, and let \(r\gt0\) be so small that every point at distance less than \(2r\) from \(\Gamma\) has a unique nearest point on \(\Gamma\). Drill the channel \(\{x\colon\operatorname{dist}(x,\Gamma)\lt r\}\), and let \(q\) be the open disk of radius \(r\) centered at \(E\); the empty part of the channel belongs to neither piece. The interface is the semicircular end cap of the channel, and the walls of the channel, at distance \(r\) from \(\Gamma\), leave its endpoints tangentially. A disk of radius \(r\) fits in the channel only when it is centered on \(\Gamma\): if its center \(c\) is at distance \(d\in(0,r]\) from \(\Gamma\), then the point at distance \(r'\in(r-d,r)\) beyond \(c\), on the ray from the nearest point of \(\Gamma\) through \(c\), is at distance \(d+r'\gt r\) from \(\Gamma\). A slide moves the center along a segment and a turn along a circular arc, and \(\Gamma\) contains neither, since its curvature is not constant along any part of it; the only exception is the spin about \(E\), which does not displace the disk. On the other hand, translating \(q\) so that its center runs along \(\Gamma\) takes it out of the board without overlap.

Exercises
  1. Suppose that the normals of the interface lie in no closed half-plane bounded by a line through the origin. Why does this alone not imply that the pieces are interlocked?
    Solution

    This assumption only rules out slides; a piece whose normals point in all directions may still be turned. For instance, a round head without a neck has normals in no closed half-plane, as in the previous exercise, but from the center of the head the interface is at constant distance, so by statement of Theorem 2 the head can be turned about its center. This puzzle is discussed in Moving a piece vs. taking it out.

  2. Show that the wedged tooth and the drilled channel are not in clean contact: at which points, and why, does the definition fail?
    Solution

    In the wedged tooth, the interface is the segment \(AB\), whose endpoints are \(A\) and \(B\). Continue \(\gamma\) beyond \(A\) by its tangent ray, i.e. by the line through \(A\) and \(B\). Near \(A\), the piece \(p\) lies below this line, but also above it, between the line and the side of the slot through \(A\). So the continued curve does not separate \(p\) from \(q\) near \(A\), and the same happens at \(B\).

    In the drilled channel, the interface is the semicircular end cap of the channel. At each of its two endpoints, the walls of the channel leave the cap tangentially, so the wall through that endpoint, a boundary arc of \(p\) other than \(\gamma\), is tangent to the continued curve \(\hat\gamma\) there. This is excluded by the second condition. (The first condition holds: near the other points of the cap, the disk lies on one side and \(p\) on the other.)

  3. In the wedged tooth, where is it used that the base angles are less than \(60^\circ\)?
    Solution

    In a triangle, the longest side is opposite the largest angle. If the base angles of the taller triangle are less than \(60^\circ\), then its third angle is greater than \(60^\circ\), hence greater than both base angles, so the base \(AB\) is its longest side, and \(A\) and \(B\) are the only two points of the taller triangle at distance \(|AB|\) from each other. So as long as the base of \(q\) lies in the taller triangle, it lies at \(AB\); hence it cannot move, and neither can \(q\). If the base angles were \(60^\circ\) or more, the other two sides of the taller triangle would be at least as long as \(AB\), and the argument would break down.

Some observations

The head is not interlocked: from the center of the head the interface is at constant distance, so by statement of Theorem 2, \(q\) can be turned about the center. A turn about the center keeps \(q\) inside its disk, so it never takes \(q\) out. No other single slide or turn moves \(q\) at all, since the normals of the interface sweep more than a half-circle, and from every pivot other than the center the interface both recedes and approaches. Nevertheless, \(q\) can be taken out: turn it about its center by about a quarter turn, and then slide it out obliquely.

Theorem 2 only concerns the beginning of a motion: it compares the velocity of the motion at time zero with the normals of the interface. Whether \(q\) can be taken out depends on the whole motion, and on the shape of the pieces away from their initial position.

A natural question is whether a piece that can be taken out can always be taken out by finitely many slides and turns. Without clean contact, the answer is no: in the drilled channel of Figure 6, every slide, and every turn other than a spin about the center of the disk, carries its center off the curve along which the channel was drilled, so no finite sequence of slides and turns displaces the disk, although it can be taken out. We do not know the answer for pieces in clean contact.

In the idealized puzzle, moving a piece and taking it out are the same thing: by Theorem 1, if \(q\) can be moved at all, then a single slide carries it away to infinity.

A disk in a round hole. Here is an extreme case. Let \(q\) be a disk, fitted into a round hole of \(p\). The disk can be turned about its center, since this keeps it inside the hole, so by our definition the pieces are not interlocked. Yet the disk can never be taken out, or even displaced: it must stay in the hole, and a disk fits in a round hole of the same size in only one position.

Exercises
  1. Show that the trouble with the disk disappears if the puzzle is infinite and the interface is not a closed curve. Formally: let \(p\) and \(q\) be the two pieces of an infinite cut, as in Theorem 1, and suppose that \(q\) can be moved; show that there is a vector \(w\) such that \(q+sw\) does not meet \(p\) for every \(s\ge0\), and \(q+sw\neq q\) for every \(s\gt0\).
    Solution

    If \(q\) can be moved, then by Theorem 1 there is \(w\neq0\) with \(n\cdot w\ge0\) at every smooth point of \(\gamma\), and \((q+sw)\cap p=\varnothing\) for every \(s\ge0\). Suppose that \(q+sw=q\) for some \(s\gt0\). Then \(\gamma\), the boundary of \(q\), is invariant under the translation by \(sw\), so it consists of copies of an arc from a point \(x\) to \(x+sw\). Since \(n=\dot\gamma^\perp\), the condition \(n\cdot w\ge0\) says that \(\dot\gamma\cdot w^\perp\le0\). Integrating over the arc from \(x\) to \(x+sw\) gives \(sw\cdot w^\perp=0\), so \(\dot\gamma\cdot w^\perp=0\) along the arc, i.e. the arc is a segment parallel to \(w\), and \(\gamma\) is a straight line. In that case, replace \(w\) by the normal \(n\), which is constant: then \(q+sn\) is a half-plane strictly inside \(q\), so it does not meet \(p\), and it differs from \(q\) for every \(s\gt0\).

  2. Which assumption of Theorem 1 fails for the interface of the disk, and how does this allow the disk to spin?
    Solution

    The interface of the disk is a closed curve, while in Theorem 1 the interface goes off to infinity at both ends. The spin of the disk consists of turns about its center \(z\) by all small angles, and each of them maps the circle onto itself, so that the set \(q\) stays in place. A curve which is mapped onto itself by the turns about \(z\) by all small angles contains, together with each of its points \(x\), the whole circle through \(x\) centered at \(z\). But a curve going off to infinity at both ends, without crossing itself, contains no circle.

  3. By our definition, the disk in a round hole is not interlocked, although it can never leave its place. Can you think of a definition of “interlocked” which fits our intuition better? How would Theorem 2 change?
    One possible answer

    Call \(p\) and \(q\) interlocked if every admissible motion leaves the set \(q\) in place, i.e. \(g_\tau(q)=q\) for every \(\tau\). The disk must stay in the hole, and a disk fits in a round hole of the same size only in its original position; so \(g_\tau(q)=q\) for every admissible motion, and the pieces are interlocked in the new sense.

    Theorem 2 survives with a single exception. In statement , every admissible motion is constant, so it leaves \(q\) in place. In statement , a small slide moves the set \(q\), since a bounded set is not mapped onto itself by a translation. In statement , a small turn about \(z\) moves the set \(q\), unless \(q\) is mapped onto itself by all turns about \(z\), i.e. unless \(q\) is a disk or an annulus centered at \(z\), as in the round hole. In that case, if no slide moves \(q\), then the pieces are interlocked in the new sense: since \(q\) is symmetric about \(z\), the translation taking \(z\) to \(g_\tau(z)\) maps \(q\) onto the same set as \(g_\tau\), so these translations form an admissible motion, and if it were not constant, then by the argument in the proof of Theorem 1 some slide would move \(q\). So with the new definition, Theorem 2 changes only in that a disk or an annulus which no slide moves counts as interlocked, although it can be turned about its center.

Bonus: how much must the cut bend?

As we travel along the interface, its direction turns. The total angle through which it turns, counting left and right turns alike, is called the total curvature of the interface. For example, a straight cut has total curvature \(0\), and a semicircle has total curvature \(\pi\).

Formally

Let \(\gamma\) be parametrized by arclength. Along each smooth piece of \(\gamma\), write

\[ \dot\gamma=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta), \]

where the angle \(\theta\) depends smoothly on \(t\). The curvature of \(\gamma\) is \(\kappa=\dot\theta\); it is positive where \(\gamma\) turns left and negative where it turns right. Let \(\gamma(t_1),\gamma(t_2),\dots\) be the corners of \(\gamma\). The exterior angle at \(\gamma(t_i)\) is the angle \(\alpha_i\in(-\pi,\pi)\) from the incoming tangent \(\dot\gamma(t_i^-)\) to the outgoing tangent \(\dot\gamma(t_i^+)\), measured counterclockwise. The total curvature of \(\gamma\) is

\[ \int|\kappa|\,dt+\sum_i|\alpha_i|. \]

Two corners, with exterior angles \(\alpha_1\gt0\) and \(\alpha_2\lt0\). The dashed lines continue the incoming tangents.

For an infinite cut, the integral and the sum are taken over all of \(\mathbb{R}\), and the total curvature may be infinite.

Proposition

Let \(p\) and \(q\) be adjacent pieces of a jigsaw puzzle in clean contact. If \(p\) and \(q\) are interlocked, then the total curvature of their interface is greater than \(\pi\). Moreover, for every \(\varepsilon\gt0\), there are interlocked pieces whose interface has total curvature less than \(\pi+\varepsilon\).

The reason is simple: if the interface turns by at most half a turn, then its normals cover at most half a circle of directions, so a slide moves \(q\).

The bound \(\pi\) is approached by the tongue of Figure 10: a round head which is only slightly more than a semicircle, continued along its tangents to the edge of the board. The head blocks every slide and every turn except the one about its center, and the two straight walls block that turn, at no cost in bending.

Figure 10: the tongue

The slider changes the angular width of the arc, which equals the total curvature of the interface.

Proof of the Proposition

Suppose that the total curvature of \(\gamma\) is at most \(\pi\). Choose the angle \(\theta\) of \(\dot\gamma\) so that at each corner it jumps by the exterior angle. Since \(\dot\theta=\kappa\) along the smooth pieces, for smooth points \(t\lt t'\),

\[ |\theta(t')-\theta(t)|\le\int_t^{t'}|\kappa|\,du+\sum_{t\lt t_i\lt t'}|\alpha_i|\le\pi. \]

Hence there is \(a\in\mathbb{R}\) with \(a\le\theta\le a+\pi\). The normal \(n\) has angle \(\theta+\pi/2\). Let \(w\) be the unit vector of angle \(a+\pi\). Then, at every smooth point,

\[ n\cdot w=\cos\left(\theta-a-\tfrac\pi2\right)\ge0, \]

since \(|\theta-a-\pi/2|\le\pi/2\). By statement of Theorem 2, a slide in the direction \(w\) moves \(q\), so \(p\) and \(q\) are not interlocked.

For the second statement, fix \(\delta\gt0\), and let \(\Gamma\) be the arc of the unit circle of angular width \(\pi+\delta\), symmetric about the first axis and open on the left. Its tangents at its endpoints meet at the point \((-1/\sin\frac\delta2,0)\).

The pieces of the proof, for \(\delta=30^\circ\), as at the start of Figure 10. The arc \(\Gamma\) is continued along its tangents, which meet outside the board. Along \(\Gamma\), the normals point at the origin.

In a rectangular board which contains the closed unit disk but not this point, let the interface be \(\Gamma\), continued along these tangents to the edge of the board. This is the tongue of Figure 10, whose slider shows the width \(180^\circ+\delta\) of the arc. It has no corners, so its total curvature is \(\pi+\delta\). Along \(\Gamma\) the normals point at the origin, so they sweep an arc of directions of length \(\pi+\delta\), which lies in no closed half-plane bounded by a line through the origin: no slide moves \(q\). For turns, let \(z\) be a pivot. Along \(\Gamma\) we have \(\dot\gamma\cdot(\gamma-z)=-\dot\gamma\cdot z\), since \(\dot\gamma\) is perpendicular to \(\gamma\), and \(\dot\gamma\) sweeps an arc of directions of length \(\pi+\delta\). Thus if \(z\neq0\), the interface both approaches \(z\) and recedes from it along \(\Gamma\). If \(z=0\), it approaches \(z\) along the lower wall and recedes from it along the upper wall. So no turn moves \(q\) either, and by Theorem 2 the pieces are interlocked.

Letting \(\delta\to0\) proves the second statement of the Proposition.

The extremal shapes differ from those in Fenchel's theorem7, by which the total curvature of a closed curve is at least \(2\pi\), with equality only for plane convex curves. (A related theorem of Fáry8 and Milnor9 says that a knotted curve in space has total curvature greater than \(4\pi\).) Here a circular arc alone does not suffice in a bounded board: the piece can be turned about its center (see Figure 7). The straight walls of the tongue cost no bending, and block that turn.

Appendix: proof of Theorem 2

Let us restate Theorem 2.

Theorem 2

Let \(p,q\) be adjacent pieces of a jigsaw puzzle in clean contact. Parametrize their interface \(\gamma\) by arclength, so that \(q\) lies to its left, and let \(n\) be the unit normal of \(\gamma\) pointing into \(q\). For \(z\in\mathbb{R}^2\) and \(\theta\in\mathbb{R}\), let \(\rho_z(\theta)\) be the counterclockwise rotation about \(z\) by the angle \(\theta\).

  1. If a vector \(w\neq0\) satisfies \(n\cdot w\ge0\) at every smooth point of \(\gamma\), then \(q\) can be moved by a slide in the direction \(w\): there is \(s_0\gt0\) with \((q+sw)\cap p=\varnothing\) for every \(s\in[0,s_0]\).
  2. If a point \(z\in\mathbb{R}^2\) and a sign \(\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}\) satisfy \(\varepsilon\,\dot\gamma\cdot(\gamma-z)\ge0\) at every smooth point of \(\gamma\), then \(q\) can be moved by a turn about \(z\): there is \(\theta_0\gt0\) with \(\rho_z(\varepsilon\theta)(q)\cap p=\varnothing\) for every \(\theta\in[0,\theta_0]\).
  3. Otherwise, \(p\) and \(q\) are interlocked.

Throughout, \(p\) and \(q\) are adjacent pieces in clean contact. Let \(X\) be one of the vector fields

\[ X(x)=w\quad(w\neq0)\qquad\text{or}\qquad X(x)=\varepsilon(x-z)^\perp\quad(z\in\mathbb{R}^2,\ \varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}), \]

and let \(\Phi_s\) be its flow, i.e. the slide \(\Phi_s(x)=x+sw\), respectively the turn \(\Phi_s=\rho_z(\varepsilon s)\). We call a curve \(s\mapsto\Phi_s(y)\), \(s_1\le s\le s_2\), a trajectory. Statements and of Theorem 2 follow from Lemma 2 below, whose proof uses Lemma 1, and statement follows from Lemma 3.

Lemma 1

Let \(B\) be a disk, and let \(\Sigma\) be an oriented piecewise-smooth arc crossing \(B\), which divides \(B\) into two sides, \(B^+\) on its left and \(B^-\) on its right. Let \(\nu=\dot\Sigma^\perp\), and suppose that

\[ \nu\cdot X\ge0\qquad\text{at every smooth point of }\Sigma\text{ in }B.\tag{4} \]

Then no trajectory in \(B\) which starts in \(B^+\) reaches \(\Sigma\).

A turn: \(\Sigma\) and some trajectories.

Proof of Lemma 1

Suppose by contradiction that a trajectory in \(B\) leads from \(y\in B^+\) to a point \(x=\Phi_s(y)\in\Sigma\). We first replace it by a trajectory which ends in \(B^-\). The trajectory is a compact subset of the open disk \(B\), and \(B^+\) is open, so there is \(\delta\gt0\) such that the disk of radius \(\delta\) around \(y\) lies in \(B^+\), and every point at distance less than \(\delta\) from the trajectory lies in \(B\). Since \(x\) is a limit of points of \(B^-\), there is \(x'\in B^-\) with \(|x'-x|\lt\delta\). Let \(y'=\Phi_{-s}(x')\). Each \(\Phi_u\) is an isometry, so

\[ |\Phi_u(y')-\Phi_u(y)|=|y'-y|=|x'-x|\lt\delta\qquad\text{for every }u. \]

Hence \(y'\in B^+\), and the trajectory from \(y'\) to \(\Phi_s(y')=x'\) lies in \(B\) and leads from \(B^+\) to \(B^-\). Replacing \(y\) by \(y'\), we may therefore assume that \(\Phi_s(y)\in B^-\).

Put \(\ell(x)=x_1\) if \(X=w\), where we rotate the picture so that \(w=(0,1)\), and \(\ell(x)=\varepsilon|x-z|\) if \(X=\varepsilon(x-z)^\perp\). Then \(\ell\) is constant along trajectories. At a smooth point of \(\Sigma\) other than \(z\), by (2),

\[ \nu\cdot X=(\ell\circ\Sigma)'\ \text{ for a slide},\qquad \nu\cdot X=|\Sigma-z|\,(\ell\circ\Sigma)'\ \text{ for a turn}. \]

Hence \(\ell\circ\Sigma\) is differentiable, with nonnegative derivative by (4), except at the finitely many points where \(\Sigma\) has a corner or passes through \(z\). Being continuous, \(\ell\circ\Sigma\) is nondecreasing along \(\Sigma\cap B\).

Since \(y\neq z\), as the trajectory of \(z\) is constant, we have \(\nabla\ell(y)\neq0\). Since \(B\), \(B^+\) and \(B^-\) are open, there is a disk \(D\) around \(y\) such that for every \(y'\in D\), the trajectory from \(y'\) to \(\Phi_s(y')\) also lies in \(B\) and leads from \(B^+\) to \(B^-\). Such a trajectory meets \(\Sigma\), at a point where \(\ell\) takes the value \(\ell(y')\). As \(\nabla\ell(y)\neq0\), the set \(\ell(D)\) is an interval of positive length, and \(\ell\circ\Sigma\) takes every value in it. Choose \(t_-\lt t_+\) with \(\ell(\Sigma(t_-))\lt\ell(\Sigma(t_+))\), both in \(\ell(D)\). Since \(\ell\circ\Sigma\) is not constant on \([t_-,t_+]\), there is \(t_0\in(t_-,t_+)\), at which \(\Sigma\) is smooth and avoids \(z\), with \((\ell\circ\Sigma)'(t_0)\neq0\), i.e. \((\ell\circ\Sigma)'(t_0)\gt0\) and \(\nu\cdot X\gt0\) at \(\Sigma(t_0)\). Since \(\ell(\Sigma(t_0))\in\ell(D)\), we may choose \(y'\in D\) with \(\ell(y')=\ell(\Sigma(t_0))\). Since \(\ell\circ\Sigma\) is nondecreasing and \((\ell\circ\Sigma)'(t_0)\gt0\), we have \(\ell(\Sigma(t))\lt\ell(\Sigma(t_0))\) for \(t\lt t_0\) and \(\ell(\Sigma(t))\gt\ell(\Sigma(t_0))\) for \(t\gt t_0\). The trajectory of \(y'\) lies in the level set \(\{\ell=\ell(y')\}\), so it meets \(\Sigma\) only at \(\Sigma(t_0)\). As \(\nu\cdot X\gt0\) there, it crosses \(\Sigma\) from \(B^-\) to \(B^+\). But it starts in \(B^+\) and does not meet \(\Sigma\) before \(\Sigma(t_0)\), so it lies in \(B^+\) until then. This contradiction proves Lemma 1.

The level circle through \(\Sigma(t_0)\).

Lemma 2

Suppose that

\[ n\cdot X\ge0\qquad\text{at every smooth point of }\gamma.\tag{5} \]

Then there is \(s_0\gt0\) such that \(\Phi_s(q)\cap p=\varnothing\) for every \(s\in[0,s_0]\).

Proof of Lemma 2

Suppose that no such \(s_0\) exists. Then there are \(s_j\searrow0\) and \(y_j\in q\) with \(\Phi_{s_j}(y_j)\in p\). Since \(|\Phi_s(y)-y|\le s|w|\), respectively \(\le s|y-z|\), we may pass to a subsequence such that \(y_j\) and \(\Phi_{s_j}(y_j)\) converge to the same point \(x\). Then \(x\in\overline q\cap\overline p=\gamma\), and for every disk \(B\) around \(x\), the trajectory from \(y_j\) to \(\Phi_{s_j}(y_j)\) lies in \(B\) for all large \(j\).

We first show that for every point of \(\gamma\) other than an endpoint and every sufficiently small disk \(B\) around it,

\[ \text{no trajectory in }B\text{ which starts in }q\text{ reaches }\gamma.\tag{6} \]

Indeed, for such a disk \(B\), \(\gamma\cap B\) is a single arc, dividing \(B\) into two sides, and \(B\setminus\gamma\subseteq p\cup q\) by clean contact. Each side is connected, hence contained in \(p\) or in \(q\); since \(q\) lies to the left of \(\gamma\) and \(p\) meets \(B\), the left side is \(q\cap B\) and the right side is \(p\cap B\). By (5), Lemma 1 applies to \(\Sigma=\gamma\) and \(\nu=n\), and gives (6).

We now derive a contradiction, distinguishing whether or not \(x\) is an endpoint of \(\gamma\).

  • \(x\) is not an endpoint of \(\gamma\). Let \(B\) be a disk around \(x\) as in (6). For large \(j\), the trajectory from \(y_j\in q\) to \(\Phi_{s_j}(y_j)\in p\) lies in \(B\), hence meets \(\gamma\), contradicting (6).

  • \(x=x_0\) is an endpoint of \(\gamma\). Let \(\hat\gamma\) be as in the definition of clean contact, oriented like \(\gamma\), and let \(\nu=\dot{\hat\gamma}^\perp\), which agrees with \(n\) along \(\gamma\). Let \(\psi\) be the polar angle about \(x_0\), measured from the tangent ray, in the sense for which \(\nu(x_0)\) has angle \(\pi/2\); thus \(\gamma\) leaves \(x_0\) in the direction \(\psi=\pi\). By clean contact, there is a small disk \(B\) around \(x_0\) in which \(q\) lies on the side of \(\hat\gamma\) to which \(\nu\) points, and \(p\) on the other side. In \(B\), the boundaries of \(p\) and \(q\) consist of \(\gamma\) and of finitely many arcs leaving \(x_0\), none of them tangent to \(\hat\gamma\). Hence there is \(\beta\in(0,\pi/4)\) such that, after shrinking \(B\),

    \[ \begin{aligned}&\text{the arcs of }\partial q\text{ other than }\gamma\text{ lie in }\{\beta\le\psi\le\pi-\beta\},\\&\text{the arcs of }\partial p\text{ other than }\gamma\text{ lie in }\{-\pi+\beta\le\psi\le-\beta\}.\end{aligned}\tag{7} \]

    By (5) and continuity, \(\nu(x_0)\cdot X(x_0)\ge0\).

    • \(X(x_0)\neq0\). We find an arc \(\Sigma\) through \(x_0\), oriented like \(\gamma\), which divides a small disk \(B\) around \(x_0\) into two sides, with \(q\cap B\) on its left and \(p\cap B\) on its right, and which satisfies (4). Then, for large \(j\), the trajectory from \(y_j\in q\) to \(\Phi_{s_j}(y_j)\in p\) lies in \(B\), hence meets \(\Sigma\), contradicting Lemma 1. To find \(\Sigma\), we distinguish two cases.

      • \(\nu(x_0)\cdot X(x_0)\gt0\). Let \(\Sigma=\hat\gamma\). Along \(\gamma\), (4) is (5); along the tangent ray, \(\nu\cdot X\gt0\) if \(B\) is small.

      • \(\nu(x_0)\cdot X(x_0)=0\). Then \(X(x_0)\) is parallel to the tangent ray. Let \(O\) be the orbit of \(x_0\), i.e. the line or circle traced by \(x_0\) under the flow; it is tangent to the ray at \(x_0\). Let \(\Sigma\) be the curve obtained by continuing \(\gamma\) beyond \(x_0\) by the half of \(O\) which starts in the direction \(\psi=0\). Along \(O\) we have \(\nu\cdot X=0\), since \(X\) is tangent to its own orbits, so \(\Sigma\) satisfies (4).

        It remains to show that \(q\) and \(p\) lie on the two sides of \(\Sigma\). Near \(x_0\), the half of \(O\) and the region between it and the tangent ray lie in the sector \(\{|\psi|\lt\beta\}\). We show that this sector contains no point of \(p\cup q\). By (7), and since \(\gamma\) leaves \(x_0\) in the direction \(\psi=\pi\), neither half of the sector meets \(\partial p\) or \(\partial q\). So the half \(\{0\lt\psi\lt\beta\}\) is either contained in \(q\) or disjoint from it. If it were contained in \(q\), the tangent ray would be a boundary arc of \(q\) tangent to \(\hat\gamma\), which is excluded. It is also disjoint from \(p\), which lies on the other side of \(\hat\gamma\). The same argument applies to the half \(\{-\beta\lt\psi\lt0\}\). Hence \(q\) and \(p\) lie on the same sides of \(\Sigma\) as of \(\hat\gamma\).

    • \(X(x_0)=0\). Then \(z=x_0\), and \(\Phi_s=\rho_{x_0}(\varepsilon s)\) turns points about \(x_0\) by the angle \(\varepsilon s\), keeping their distance to \(x_0\). Since \(q\) lies on the side of \(\hat\gamma\) to which \(\nu\) points, and \(\hat\gamma\) is tangent at \(x_0\) to the line \(\{\psi\in\{0,\pi\}\}\), we have, if \(B\) is small,

      \[ -\beta/2\lt\psi\lt\pi+\beta/2\pmod{2\pi}\qquad\text{on }q\cap B. \]

      Let \(j\) be so large that \(s_j\le\beta/2\) and the trajectory from \(y_j\) lies in \(B\). It stays in \(\{-\beta\lt\psi\lt\pi+\beta\}\), so by (7) it does not meet \(\partial p\setminus\gamma\); and it does not meet \(x_0\). Let \(x^*\) be its first point in \(\overline p\). Since \(p\) is open and \(y_j\notin\overline p\), the point \(x^*\) lies in \(\partial p\); so it is a point of \(\gamma\) other than \(x_0\), which is smooth if \(B\) is small. Let \(B'\) be a disk around \(x^*\) as in (6). Just before reaching \(x^*\), the trajectory lies in \(B'\) and outside \(\overline p\), hence in \(q\). Since \(x^*\in\gamma\), this contradicts (6).

In all cases we have reached a contradiction, which proves Lemma 2.

Proof of . Apply Lemma 2 to \(X(x)=w\). Then (5) is the assumption \(n\cdot w\ge0\), and since \(\Phi_s(x)=x+sw\), Lemma 2 implies that \((q+sw)\cap p=\varnothing\) for every \(s\in[0,s_0]\).

Proof of . Apply Lemma 2 to \(X(x)=\varepsilon(x-z)^\perp\). By (2), \(n\cdot X=\varepsilon\,\dot\gamma\cdot(\gamma-z)\) at every smooth point of \(\gamma\), so (5) is the assumption \(\varepsilon\,\dot\gamma\cdot(\gamma-z)\ge0\). Since \(\Phi_s=\rho_z(\varepsilon s)\), Lemma 2 implies that \(\rho_z(\varepsilon s)(q)\cap p=\varnothing\) for every \(s\in[0,s_0]\), i.e. the statement with \(\theta_0=s_0\).

Lemma 3

Suppose that there exists an admissible motion of \(q\) which is not constant. Then there are \(b\in\mathbb{R}^2\) and \(\omega\in\mathbb{R}\), not both zero, such that the vector field \(V(y)=b+\omega y^\perp\) satisfies

\[ n\cdot V\ge0\qquad\text{at every smooth point of }\gamma.\tag{8} \]
Proof of Lemma 3

The proof is similar to that of statement of Theorem 1, with clean contact in place of the fact that the interface divides the plane. Let \(g_\tau\), \(0\le\tau\le1\), be an admissible motion of \(q\) which is not constant. We may assume that \(g_\tau\) is not the identity for arbitrarily small \(\tau\gt0\), for otherwise we start the motion at the last time at which \(g_\tau\) is the identity. Choose \(\tau_j\searrow0\) such that \(g_{\tau_j}\) is not the identity, and write

\[ g_{\tau_j}(y)=(\cos\theta_j)\,y+(\sin\theta_j)\,y^\perp+a_j,\qquad\text{where }\theta_j\in(-\pi,\pi]\text{ and }a_j\in\mathbb{R}^2. \]

Since \(g\) is continuous and \(g_0\) is the identity, \(\theta_j\to0\) and \(a_j\to0\), and since \(g_{\tau_j}\) is not the identity, \((a_j,\theta_j)\neq(0,0)\). Put \(r_j=\big(|a_j|^2+\theta_j^2\big)^{1/2}\), and pass to a subsequence such that \(a_j/r_j\to b\) and \(\theta_j/r_j\to\omega\). Then \(|b|^2+\omega^2=1\), and since \(\cos\theta_j=1+O(\theta_j^2)\) and \(\sin\theta_j=\theta_j+O(\theta_j^3)\),

\[ g_{\tau_j}(y)=y+r_jV(y)+o(r_j)\qquad\text{uniformly for }y\text{ in compact sets}.\tag{9} \]

Assume by contradiction that \(n\cdot V(x)\lt0\) at some smooth point \(x\) of \(\gamma\). Since \(n\) and \(V\) are continuous along each smooth arc of \(\gamma\), we may assume that \(x\) is not an endpoint of \(\gamma\). Write \(x=\gamma(t)\) and \(n=n(t)\), and put

\[ e=\alpha n+V(x),\qquad\text{where }\alpha=-\tfrac12\,n\cdot V(x)\gt0, \]

so that \(e\cdot n=\tfrac12\,n\cdot V(x)\lt0\); in particular \(e\neq0\). Choose \(\theta\in(0,\pi/2)\) with \(e\cdot n\lt-|e|\sin\theta\), and for \(\rho\gt0\) consider the sectors

\[ S_\pm=\{x+v\colon 0\lt|v|\lt\rho,\ \pm v\cdot n\gt|v|\sin\theta\}. \]

We show that \(S_+\subseteq q\) and \(S_-\subseteq p\) if \(\rho\) is small. Since

\[ \gamma(t+\sigma)=x+\sigma\dot\gamma(t)+o(\sigma),\qquad|\dot\gamma(t)|=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\dot\gamma(t)\cdot n=0, \]

there is \(\sigma_0\gt0\) such that for \(0\lt|\sigma|\lt\sigma_0\), the vector \(v=\gamma(t+\sigma)-x\) is nonzero and satisfies \(|v\cdot n|\lt|v|\sin\theta\), i.e. \(\gamma(t+\sigma)\) lies in neither sector. The points \(\gamma(t+\sigma)\) with \(|\sigma|\ge\sigma_0\) form a compact set, which does not contain \(x\) since \(\gamma\) has no self-intersections. Hence, if \(\rho\) is smaller than the distance from \(x\) to this set, neither sector meets \(\gamma\). Let \(B\) be the disk of radius \(\rho\) about \(x\). By clean contact, if \(\rho\) is small, then \(B\setminus\gamma\subseteq p\cup q\), and, as in the proof of (6), the points of \(B\setminus\gamma\) on the left of \(\gamma\) lie in \(q\) and those on its right lie in \(p\). Each sector is a connected subset of \(B\setminus\gamma\), so it lies on one side of \(\gamma\). For small \(\varepsilon\gt0\), the point \(x+\varepsilon n\) lies in \(S_+\) and on the left of \(\gamma\), and \(x-\varepsilon n\) lies in \(S_-\) and on the right of \(\gamma\). Hence

\[ S_+\subseteq q\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_-\subseteq p. \]

Left: the disk \(B\) about \(x\) misses the rest of the interface. Right: the sectors \(S_+\subseteq q\) and \(S_-\subseteq p\) in \(B\); the motion \(g_{\tau_j}\) carries \(x_j\in S_+\) into \(S_-\).

Now put \(x_j=x+\alpha r_jn\). For large \(j\), \(0\lt\alpha r_j\lt\rho\), so \(x_j\in S_+\subseteq q\). Since \(V\) is affine, \(V(x_j)=V(x)+O(r_j)\), so by (9),

\[ g_{\tau_j}(x_j)=x+r_je+o(r_j). \]

Thus \(v_j=g_{\tau_j}(x_j)-x\) satisfies \(|v_j|=r_j|e|+o(r_j)\) and \(v_j\cdot n+|v_j|\sin\theta=r_j\big(e\cdot n+|e|\sin\theta\big)+o(r_j)\). Since \(e\neq0\) and \(e\cdot n+|e|\sin\theta\lt0\), for large \(j\) we get \(0\lt|v_j|\lt\rho\) and \(-v_j\cdot n\gt|v_j|\sin\theta\), i.e. \(g_{\tau_j}(x_j)\in S_-\subseteq p\). Hence \(g_{\tau_j}(q)\) meets \(p\), which contradicts the assumption that \(g\) is admissible.

Proof of . Suppose that the hypotheses of and fail for every \(w\), \(z\) and \(\varepsilon\), and that there exists an admissible motion of \(q\) which is not constant. Let \(b\), \(\omega\) and \(V\) be as in Lemma 3. If \(\omega=0\), then by (8), \(w=b\neq0\) satisfies \(n\cdot w\ge0\) at every smooth point of \(\gamma\). If \(\omega\neq0\), then \(V(y)=\omega(y-z)^\perp\), where \(z=b^\perp/\omega\), and by (2) and (8), \(\varepsilon\,\dot\gamma\cdot(\gamma-z)\ge0\) at every smooth point of \(\gamma\), where \(\varepsilon\) is the sign of \(\omega\). Both contradict the assumption. Hence every admissible motion is constant, i.e. \(p\) and \(q\) are interlocked.
